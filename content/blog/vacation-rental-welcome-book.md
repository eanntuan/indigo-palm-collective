<p>Most vacation rental welcome books fail in one of two ways. The first: a 40-page Canva PDF packed with every possible piece of information, formatted beautifully, and opened by approximately no one. The second: a sticky note on the fridge with the wifi password and a vague "text us if you need anything." Guests muddle through and occasionally leave reviews that mention they couldn't figure out the pool heater.</p>

<p>We've hosted 191 stays across our three Coachella Valley properties, and we've tried versions of both. What works sits squarely in the middle: a focused, scannable guide that covers the ten things guests genuinely need, in the order they need them.</p>

<p>Here's the template we use, what we've cut, and why.</p>

<h2>The 10 Sections That Belong in Every Welcome Book</h2>

<h3>1. Wifi and Streaming Logins</h3>

<p>Put this first. Not second, not buried in a section called "Technology." First. Guests want to connect before they've even set their bags down, and if they have to hunt for the password, that friction is the first thing they remember about check-in. We include the network name, password, and login credentials for Netflix and any other streaming accounts on the very first page, in a font size that's readable without glasses.</p>

<h3>2. Check-in and Check-out Instructions</h3>

<p>Guests need specific information, not approximations. Include exact check-in and check-out times (we're 4 PM in and 11 AM out), the key code or lockbox combination, which door to use, and where to park. If parking has any quirks, spell them out. At our Indio properties, we note which spots are reserved and where overflow street parking sits.</p>

<h3>3. How Things Work</h3>

<p>This section saves the most "how do I..." messages. Cover the pool heater (how to turn it on, how long it takes to warm up), the hot tub controls, the outdoor kitchen, the smart TV inputs, the thermostat, and anything else that doesn't work the way guests expect. At Terra Luz, the Kahlo blue pool has a heater that takes about 24 hours to reach temperature, so we tell guests to turn it on the night before they want to use it. That one sentence saves a lot of disappointment.</p>

<h3>4. House Rules</h3>

<p>Be direct. Don't wrap rules in corporate language or hedge everything into softness. Guests respect clarity. Ours cover noise (quiet hours 10 PM to 8 AM), gatherings (no events or parties beyond the registered guest count), and pets (allowed at Terra Luz and The Sundune with prior approval, not at The Cozy Cactus). If there's a consequence for a broken rule, say so plainly. Guests who want to follow the rules, which is most of them, will appreciate knowing exactly what they are.</p>

<h3>5. Emergency Contacts</h3>

<p>A short list: host phone number, the best way to reach us and our typical response time, the nearest urgent care (for our Indio properties that's Desert Care Network on Highway 111), and local non-emergency police if needed. We also note who to call if something breaks versus who to call if there's a safety issue. Most guests will never use this section. The ones who do will be very glad it's there.</p>

<img src="/blog/images/cc-nook-vignette.webp" alt="Cozy Cactus vacation rental interior nook in Indio California" loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="800" height="534">

<h3>6. The Neighborhood</h3>

<p>Closest grocery store, closest pharmacy, closest gas station, a coffee shop worth going to. Guests are often arriving from long drives or flights and just need to know where things are. For our Indio properties we point guests to Stater Bros. on Jefferson for groceries, CVS nearby for pharmacy runs, and Lighthouse Coffee in the East Valley for a solid morning cup. Keep it to five or six practical stops, not a full city guide.</p>

<h3>7. Local Restaurant Picks</h3>

<p>Your favorites, not just what Yelp surfaces first. Include the type of food, the neighborhood, and one sentence on why you like it. For our Indio properties we mention El Rodeo for the carnitas, Rio Azul for when guests want to stay close and eat well, and Chef Tanya's Kitchen for the plant-based crowd. Guests trust a host rec far more than they trust a star rating, and this is one of the lowest-effort ways to add value. Understanding <a href="/blog/airbnb-hosting-lessons/">what guests care about</a> comes down to this: they want to feel like a local for a few days, not a tourist with a phone.</p>

<h3>8. Festival and Event Info</h3>

<p>For properties near major events, this section earns its place. Terra Luz and The Cozy Cactus are walking distance to Empire Polo Club, which hosts Coachella and Stagecoach. We tell guests that, we tell them which entrance is closest, and we mention that Indian Palms Country Club (just around the corner from The Cozy Cactus) has a public golf course if they want to play a round before the gates open. For guests staying during festival weeks, this is the section they read twice.</p>

<h3>9. Checkout Checklist</h3>

<p>Five items, maximum. Ours: lock all doors and windows, start the dishwasher, strip the beds and leave linens in the laundry room, take all food out of the fridge, and return keys or ensure the lockbox is secured. A simple list like this reduces the chance of guests leaving something behind or forgetting a step, and it sets the right expectation without reading like a legal document. Keeping a <a href="/blog/staging-vacation-rental-photo-shoot/">photo-ready presentation</a> between stays starts with guests knowing exactly what you expect at departure.</p>

<h3>10. How to Reach Us</h3>

<p>Restate your phone number, your preferred contact method (for us it's text, not Airbnb messaging, for anything time-sensitive), and roughly how fast we respond. We tell guests we typically reply within an hour during daylight hours and within three hours overnight. That sets a clear expectation, and we meet it consistently. A lot of our 4.98 and 4.97 ratings at Terra Luz and The Cozy Cactus come back to communication.</p>

<h2>What to Leave Out</h2>

<p>Three things that don't belong in a welcome book, no matter how tempting they are to include.</p>

<p>Your full rental history and origin story. Guests don't need to know when you bought the property, what it looked like before renovation, or what inspired the design concept. That content has a place on a blog or a listing description. In the welcome book it just takes up space and dilutes the practical information guests are looking for.</p>

<p>Lengthy legal disclaimers. Liability language, damage policies, and cancellation terms belong in the rental agreement guests signed before booking. Repeating them in the welcome book signals distrust and makes the whole document feel defensive. Keep legal language where it has teeth and leave the welcome book for hospitality.</p>

<p>Enthusiastic filler language. "We LOVE having guests!!!" and "We know you're going to have the most AMAZING stay!!!" add nothing. They read as performative rather than genuine, and they pad the document with words guests have to skim past to find the pool heater instructions. Write the way you'd talk to someone you just met and want to help out.</p>

<h2>Format Matters Too</h2>

<p>We use both a laminated printed version in the kitchen and a digital version linked in the booking confirmation. The printed copy catches the guests who don't read their email before arrival. The digital version helps guests who want to look something up from the pool. Neither replaces the other. For the printed version, one or two pages works; for the digital, clear section headers let people jump to what they need in under 30 seconds.</p>

<p>The goal of the welcome book isn't to impress anyone with its production value. The goal is that no guest ever has to wonder how something works, where something is, or how to reach you. If the guide does that, it's working. If guests are texting you to ask things the guide should have covered, it needs another pass.</p>
