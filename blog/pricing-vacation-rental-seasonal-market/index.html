How to Price a Vacation Rental in a Seasonal Market

By Eann Tuan · July 16, 2026 · 6 min read

A flat nightly rate is wrong almost every night. Set it based on what you want to earn and you'll be too cheap during Coachella weekend and too expensive in July, losing money in both directions at once. The Coachella Valley is one of the clearest examples of this problem in the US, because the swings are so sharp and so predictable that there's really no excuse for getting it wrong.

We run three properties: Terra Luz (3BR/2BA, Indio), The Cozy Cactus (3BR/2BA, Indio), and The Sundune (2BR/2BA, Palm Springs). Across 191 reviews and a few years of adjustments, here's how we think about pricing across this market.

Festival Weekends: Set These Manually, Early

Coachella and Stagecoach are the highest-revenue days of the year for Terra Luz and The Cozy Cactus. Both properties are walking distance to the Empire Polo Club, so the demand during festival weekends is real and sustained, not just noise in the algorithm. Groups planning festival trips book 6-9 months in advance, which means your pricing needs to be live and correct before most people are even thinking about it.

We don't use dynamic pricing tools for festival dates. Tools like PriceLabs read local occupancy signals well in normal conditions, but they consistently underestimate demand during major events because the signal arrives too late and too gradually. By the time the algorithm sees high demand, your best guests have already booked somewhere else. Set festival weekend rates manually, set them early, and don't second-guess yourself in February when the calendar still looks open.

Minimum stay of 3+ nights for festival weekends. Without it, you'll get a Friday check-in and a Sunday check-out that blocks the surrounding nights and nets you less than a full weekend group would have paid.

Peak Shoulder Season: Use Dynamic Pricing With a Floor

October through May, excluding festival weekends, is the main booking window. The Coachella Valley is busy with snowbirds, golfers, and long weekend travelers from LA. Weather is good. Demand is consistent but not explosive, which is exactly where dynamic pricing earns its keep.

We use PriceLabs for this range. It reads local occupancy signals better than we can do manually, adjusts weekday/weekend splits automatically, and catches demand bumps around events we'd otherwise miss (the BNP Paribas tennis at Indian Wells, long weekends, local events). Set a rate floor, though. Without one, a slow weeknight in March can drop below what it costs you to host a stay, and that's not a booking you want.

Minimum stay of 2 nights for weekends during peak shoulder season. Weekday minimums can stay at 1 night to keep the calendar moving.

Dead Summer: Drop the Rate and the Minimum

Late June through August, temperatures hit 115 degrees. Demand drops sharply, and fighting it with pricing is a losing strategy. The empty calendar costs more than the discounted rate.

We drop nightly rates and minimum stays during summer. One-night and two-night bookings are fine. The guests who do book desert stays in summer are usually locals, regional travelers with a specific reason to be in the valley, or deal-hunters who planned ahead. They're often great guests, because they knew exactly what they were getting into. Don't try to hold peak rates through July. It doesn't work.

The Sundune in Palm Springs holds up slightly better in summer than our Indio properties, partly because Palm Springs has more walkable infrastructure and attracts a slightly different guest type. But all three see reduced demand. Summer is the time to fill the gaps with flexible pricing, not to wait for a guest willing to pay January rates in August.

Indio vs. Palm Springs: Price Them Differently

Terra Luz and The Cozy Cactus are event-driven. Their peak is tightly tied to Coachella, Stagecoach, and the BNP Paribas tournament. Palm Springs, where The Sundune is, has more year-round traffic from LA weekenders, architecture tourists, and the general draw of the Palm Springs brand. It's less spikey.

This matters because if you're comparing your Indio property to your Palm Springs property and wondering why they have different occupancy in November, the answer isn't the properties. It's the markets. Indio shoulder season requires a bit more active pricing work to stay competitive. Palm Springs is more forgiving.

What PriceLabs Is Good For and Where It Falls Short

We've used PriceLabs across all three properties for a few years. The honest summary: it's excellent at the routine work and unreliable at the edges.

Good: day-of adjustments to capture last-minute bookings, weekday/weekend splits, reading local occupancy trends, auto-adjusting around smaller events we didn't manually calendar. This is the majority of your calendar, and it handles it better than manual management.

Not good: major events. Coachella, Stagecoach, BNP Paribas, any festival or event that drives a sudden regional demand spike. The tool models demand based on historical data and current market signals, but it can't fully see the spike coming until it's already here. For those specific dates, set prices manually and lock them.

Across 191 reviews and a few years of watching what affects guest behavior, pricing is one of the things that feels strategic but is mostly just bookkeeping. Get the big windows right (festival, shoulder, summer), let the tool handle the middle, and don't over-optimize.

Setting Your Floor: Based on Costs, Not Hope

A floor rate is the minimum nightly rate you'll accept. It should be based on your real costs: your carrying cost per night (mortgage or rent), cleaning fee, utilities, and platform fees. Not based on what you're hoping to net, and not based on what your neighbor charges.

For a 3BR in Indio during dead summer, our floor is lower than most hosts are comfortable with. That's fine. A booked night at the floor beats an empty calendar at a rate nobody's willing to pay. Where a lot of hosts go wrong is setting the floor based on aspirational income, then watching July and August go completely dark while telling themselves they'd rather leave it empty than "give it away."

Set the floor at your break-even. Then make pricing decisions above that based on what the market will bear.

The Cleaning Fee Question

A high cleaning fee filters out short-stay bookings and increases revenue per stay, but reduces overall occupancy. A low cleaning fee increases short stays and turnovers but keeps the calendar busier. Neither is wrong, but the math matters.

We keep cleaning fees consistent across the calendar and raise the nightly rate instead of inflating the cleaning fee to artificially lower the displayed nightly rate. Guests notice the cleaning fee when they're comparing options, and a cleaning fee that looks out of line with the nightly rate raises questions before the booking even happens.

Direct Booking and What the Fee Math Looks Like

When guests book direct at indigopalm.co, there's no Airbnb 20% service fee. On a $1,250 booking that's $250 the guest keeps before taxes. You can pass some of that savings to the guest with a modest direct booking discount and still net more than you would through Airbnb. We've written out the full math on why direct booking saves guests real money if you want to see how it works in practice.

For hosts trying to build a direct booking channel, pricing is a lever here. Your direct rate can be slightly lower than your Airbnb rate because you're not paying platform fees, and you can make that visible to repeat guests without confusing your Airbnb listing.

Checking Your Work

Pricing is a live experiment, not a set-it-and-forget situation. Check your calendar occupancy monthly and compare it to competitors on Airbnb. If you're filling 90 days out, your floor is probably too low. If you have open dates 30 days out in peak season, your rate is probably too high for that window.

The goal isn't 100% occupancy. It's revenue maximization across the whole calendar, which sometimes means leaving a bad-fit booking opportunity empty and waiting for a better one. That calculus changes completely between festival weekends and dead summer, which is the whole point: price each window for what it is, not for what you need it to be.
