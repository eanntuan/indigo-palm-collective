How to Respond to a Bad Airbnb Review Without Losing Stars

By Eann Tuan · July 14, 2026 · 5 min read

A bad review is not the problem. A bad response is. We've read hundreds of host responses across Airbnb over the years, and most of them do one of three things: get defensive, apologize in a way that sounds scripted and hollow, or write such a long rebuttal that future guests finish it thinking "this host sounds exhausting." All three approaches make things worse.

The thing most hosts miss is that their response to a negative review is not for the person who left it. That guest is gone. The response is for every future guest who reads it, and there will be a lot of them.

We run three properties in the Coachella Valley: Terra Luz (3BR/2BA, Indio, 4.98 stars), The Cozy Cactus (3BR/2BA, also Indio, 4.97 stars), and The Sundune (2BR/2BA, Palm Springs, 4.93 stars). Across all three we've collected 191 Airbnb reviews, and we've had to respond to a handful of negative ones. What that experience revealed is that a calm, specific, honest response to a bad review can increase bookings, because it signals to future guests that you're the kind of host who takes things seriously without losing their composure.

Here's the four-part framework we use.

1. Acknowledge, but don't apologize for things that weren't wrong

If the complaint is valid, say so clearly and specifically. "You're right that the pool heater wasn't working on arrival, and that was our failure to catch before your stay" is far more credible than a vague "we're sorry your experience fell short." Specificity signals accountability. Future guests trust it.

If the complaint is subjective or doesn't match reality, you can acknowledge the experience without conceding the underlying point. Something like: "We're sorry the noise level wasn't what you expected. Our listing notes that this is a residential neighborhood near the festival grounds, which does bring some activity during Coachella season." You're not agreeing that the listing was misrepresented. You're just showing you heard them.

One phrase to avoid at all costs: "We're so sorry you felt that way." It reads as dismissive and slightly passive-aggressive, and any guest with decent instincts will clock it immediately.

2. Name one specific thing you've changed or clarified

Even when a review isn't fully fair, name one concrete action you've taken in response. "We've updated the listing to clarify pool heating schedules" or "We've added a note to the welcome book about the AC unit on the east side of the house." It doesn't have to be a major overhaul. What matters is that it's real and specific.

This signals something important to future guests: you respond to feedback actively, and you're always trying to make the stay better. That's a quality most people actively want in a host.

3. Write your last sentence for future guests, not the reviewer

Your closing line should not be directed at the person who left the review. Don't promise they'll love it next time, and don't beg them to come back. Instead, write directly to the reader who's thinking about booking.

Something like: "Our properties are built for guests who want a quiet base for the festival season with easy access to the polo grounds. If that's you, we'd love to host you." That's not for the unhappy guest. That's for the next ten people who read the thread and are trying to decide if you're the kind of host they can trust.

4. Keep it short

If your response is longer than the original review, you've already lost. Aim for three to five sentences. Concise reads as confident. Long reads as defensive, even if every word is reasonable. Most hosts go wrong here because they feel the need to address every point, explain every decision, and prove they were right. Resist that instinct entirely.

What not to do

Don't use a copy-pasted template response for every review. "We strive to provide every guest with a 5-star experience and are sorry we fell short" is the hospitality equivalent of hold music. Guests can see when you've done this, and it signals that you're going through the motions rather than listening.

Don't get into a factual dispute in the public response. If a guest says the hot tub was broken and it wasn't, that's a conversation for Airbnb support, not for the comment thread. Fighting it out publicly makes you look combative regardless of who's right.

Don't reference the reviewer negatively or hint that they're being dishonest. Even if they are. Future guests can't verify who's telling the truth, and the host who questions a guest's credibility in public always looks worse than the one who stays calm.

The ratings math, briefly

At 191 reviews across our three properties, a single 3-star rating barely moves the needle on a 4.97 or 4.98 average. The math just doesn't work that way at scale. What does move the needle is whether your response to that review makes the next ten guests more or less likely to book.

Think of every response as a message to your next thousand readers, not to the one person who left the review. That reframe changes how you write it entirely.

Prevention is still the real answer

Most bad reviews trace back to one missed thing, not a systemic failure. A pool heater that was already flagging. An AC filter that needed to be replaced a week earlier. A detail in the welcome book that wasn't clear enough. Very few guests leave a 3-star review because the entire experience was bad. Usually it's one specific moment that soured an otherwise fine stay.

The best version of review management is just not giving guests a reason to leave a bad one. We use a detailed turnover checklist before every stay to catch the things that would otherwise slip through, and we put a lot of work into our welcome book to set expectations clearly before guests ever arrive. Most complaints come from surprises, so the fewer surprises, the fewer complaints.

A well-written response buys you credibility with future guests. A tight operation means you're writing fewer of them.
